Every serious account of an altered state agrees on one thing before it agrees on anything else: the clock stops mattering. The astronaut watching a sunrise every ninety minutes loses the habit of naming days. The psychonaut six hours into a session has no use for a watch. The free diver at ninety feet, breath held past the point of reason, is not thinking about tomorrow.

Different instruments, different risks — the same report every time. There is no later from where they are. There is only now, and now turns out to be enormous.

its always today is a field series built around that report. Not the postcard version — the overview-effect photo, the ego-death anecdote at a dinner party — but the instrument readings underneath it: what actually happens to a person's sense of time, self, and stakes when they go far enough past the edge of ordinary experience to lose their place in the calendar.

The people who report this are not one type. They're pilots and monks, chemists and free divers, grievers and endurance athletes, children on a swing and old men on a ventilator. What they share isn't the method. It's the finding — presence isn't a mood you arrange for yourself on a good morning. It's a place you can be sent to, on purpose or by accident, and once you've been there, the ordinary experience of time never quite closes back over you the same way.

the roster

Psychonauts

Explorers who go inward on purpose — through breathwork, fasting, sleep deprivation, silent retreat, or compounds old and new — chasing a state where the self gets quiet enough to notice what's underneath it. Mapped as often by philosophers as by chemists: dissolution of the ego, a felt sense of contacting something realer than the room you're sitting in.

Astronauts

Explorers who go outward on purpose and arrive at the same report from the opposite direction. Documented for decades as the overview effect: watching the planet from orbit collapses separate nations, separate problems, separate selves, into one obviously fragile thing turning in the dark. No compound required — just enough distance to see the whole of it at once.

Everything between

Free divers past the point the body starts shutting down non-essential functions. Endurance athletes deep into a race, past bargaining, into what some call flow and some call the pain cave. Surgeons in hour six of an operation. Grievers in the first minutes after a death in the room. None of them set out to study consciousness. All of them came back with the same field note.

its always today runs on the same method as everything else in the writing section here: research and drafting as a way of actually working through a question, not just packaging an answer once it's already settled.

Written and researched by Nick Conenna, an applied AI architect, alongside Claude. New logs are added as they're reported, not on a schedule — the series moves at the pace of the research, which felt like the right way to build something about presence.
