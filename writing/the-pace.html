FACT

The International Prototype Kilogram — a cylinder of platinum-iridium — was kept beneath three nested glass bells in a vault at Sèvres, France, behind three keys held by three separate parties. For one hundred and thirty years every kilogram on earth was a copy of it. In 2019 it was retired, and the kilogram was redefined against the Planck constant.

In 1834 the Palace of Westminster burned, destroying the imperial standards of the yard and the pound. Replacements took more than a decade.

A nautical mile was defined as one minute of arc of latitude. A circle contains 21,600 arcminutes. The earth is therefore 21,600 nautical miles around. In the Surya Siddhanta, a human day is reckoned at 21,600 breaths.

In 1897 Nikola Tesla destroyed his royalty agreement with the Westinghouse Electric Company, forfeiting what would have been one of the largest fortunes of the twentieth century, to keep alternating current alive. He died in a hotel room on West 34th Street in 1943.

In 1901 J.P. Morgan financed the tower at Wardenclyffe, believing he was funding transatlantic wireless telegraphy. When he understood that Tesla intended wireless power, he withdrew.

In November 1910 six men traveled to Jekyll Island, Georgia, using only their first names, under cover of a duck-hunting trip, to draft the legislation that became the Federal Reserve System.

The first block of the Bitcoin ledger, mined 3 January 2009, contains an embedded reference to a London newspaper's front page that morning concerning a second bank bailout. It cannot be edited. It has been readable by anyone for seventeen years.

In 1994 Peter Shor published an algorithm that solves, on a sufficiently large quantum computer, the two mathematical problems on which nearly all current public-key cryptography depends. The successor standards are selected through a public process administered by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology.

All of the above is true.

A NOTE ON UNITS

Every unit in this book that comes from the body is real.

The digit is a finger's width. The palm is four digits. The span is the reach from thumb to little finger. The cubit is the forearm, elbow to fingertip. The foot is a foot. The pace is a double step, and mille passus — a thousand of them — is the Roman mile, which is why every highway sign in America still counts a marching soldier's legs. The fathom is from the Old English fæthm, meaning embrace: the span of a man's outstretched arms, which is how sailors measured the depth of the sea.

The reader is not required to remember any of this.

The protagonist cannot forget it.

PART ONE

The Sentence in the Ground

Prologue

Wardenclyffe · Shoreham, New York · 4:11 a.m.

Dr. Ambrose Teague had eleven minutes left and he intended to spend them walking.

The brick laboratory behind him was the last building Stanford White designed before a jealous husband shot him in the face at Madison Square Garden. Teague had always thought that was the honest epigraph for this place. Everything here had ended badly and none of it had been wrong.

The tower was gone — sold for scrap in 1917 against a hotel bill. But the foundation remained under the weeds. An octagon of concrete, and beneath it the shaft, sunk a hundred and twenty feet into Long Island sand because Tesla had insisted that to grip the earth you must first get your hand around it.

Behind him, the woman was still coming. She had not hurried once.

That was the detail he could not stop noticing, even with his shirt going warm and heavy against his ribs. She walked at a fixed rate. She had walked at that rate through the parking lot, through the treeline, through the twenty minutes it took her to find him, and she was walking at it still. A metronome inside a person. Teague had spent forty-one years measuring things and he knew a calibrated instrument when he saw one.

"Doctor." Her voice was pleasant. "You've made your point. Where is it?"

He did not answer. He put his left foot down, then his right, and he counted.

One.

He had taught this. Fifty years of undergraduates, and the first thing he taught them was that before the meter, before the yard, before any of it, a man measured the world by walking across it. Mille passus. A thousand paces. That is all a Roman mile ever was — a soldier's legs, doubled, counted under a sun that did not care.

Twelve. Thirteen.

They would find him. The alarm had gone out at 3:52 and Suffolk County was nine minutes away on a bad night. They would find him and photograph everything and understand none of it, because they would measure the scene in feet, and feet were the wrong instrument for this message.

Thirty-one.

The woman was closer. She had stopped asking.

There was exactly one person alive who would look at his body in the weeds and ask the correct question. Not what killed him. Not what did he touch.

How far did he walk.

Fifty-two. Fifty-three.

His vision was doing something at the edges. He thought about his wife, dead six years. He thought about the fact that he had never once been on time for anything that mattered. He thought about the boy — the boy was fifty now, God, fifty — who used to pace off the driveway in Rocky Point and get it right to the inch.

Sixty-seven.

He went down at sixty-eight, hard, and the sand took his palms. He did not try to rise. He got two fingers into the wet dirt and drew, carefully, a single character. Not a letter. Not a number.

The woman arrived and stood over him, breathing the way a person breathes at rest.

"That isn't an answer," she said.

Teague smiled at the sand. "It is if you can read it."

She waited until she was certain, which took another forty seconds, and then walked back to the road at exactly the rate she had come.

She never once looked at the ground behind her.

1

Boulder, Colorado · 6:11 a.m. Mountain

Zeno Canna woke at eleven minutes past six because that was when he woke.

There was no alarm in the house. No clock in the bedroom, no watch on the nightstand, no phone left face-up where a screen could tell him something his body already knew. He lay still and let the count arrive — four sleep cycles at ninety minutes, plus the twenty-six he had lost lying awake over the grant renewal — and then opened his eyes knowing it was between 6:09 and 6:14 and that he had slept enough.

He drank sixteen ounces of water, because sixteen was the amount that made his hands read true. He stood at the window and took the first eight breaths of the day slowly enough to count them.

Fifteen a minute. Nine hundred an hour. Twenty-one thousand six hundred in a day.

The same number, precisely, as the nautical miles around the earth.

He had said that out loud once, on a panel in Delft, and a Dutch physicist had laughed at him, and then gone quiet, and then not spoken again for the rest of the hour.

The phone rang at 6:14.

Area code 631. Suffolk County. Long Island. The only place on earth Zeno Canna had promised himself he would not go back to.

He looked at it for four breaths. Then he answered, and a woman with a flat administrative voice asked him to confirm that he was the son of Dr. Ambrose Teague, and Zeno said the sentence he had been not-saying for thirty years.

"Stepson. And he'd correct you on that."

There was the pause people make before the thing.

He listened. He put a hand flat against the window glass and found it was fifty-one degrees outside, give or take two, and that his pulse had gone from fifty-eight to a hundred and four in under three seconds. He catalogued both, because cataloguing was what he had instead of screaming.

"Detective," he said, when she had finished. "Before anyone moves him."

"Sir, the scene is already—"

"Before anyone moves him." His own voice surprised him. "One measurement. Your people won't take it, because they won't know it's there."

"What measurement?"

Zeno looked out at the Flatirons going pink and understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, exactly what his stepfather had spent the last minutes of his life doing.

"How far he walked," he said.

2

210 feet above Water Street, Manhattan · 6:40 a.m. Eastern

Aldrich Vane took his meetings standing, and he took this one alone.

The room had no screens. That was deliberate and expensive; on this floor, in this year, a room without screens cost more than screens. There was a table, four chairs nobody used, and on the wall a single object under museum glass: a brass yard, one of the standards struck in London in 1845 after fire destroyed the imperial original. Metal cut, cured, and compared against nothing but itself.

He liked to stand near it while he did difficult things.

"Say the number again," he said.

The voice on the line said it again.

Vane closed his eyes. Four million coins. Perhaps six. Keys already lying in the open on a public ledger, mirrored across ninety countries, every one of them exposed the moment somebody's machine grew large enough. Not stolen. Not hacked. Solved. A distinction that mattered enormously to lawyers and not at all to markets.

"Timeline."

"Estimates vary."

"Everything varies. Give me the one you'd bet your own money on."

A pause. "Under four years. Possibly under two."

Vane opened his eyes and looked at the brass.

People assumed he loved money. It was such a small idea that he had stopped correcting it around his fortieth birthday. He had watched his grandfather's savings evaporate in a country that no longer existed, in a currency whose name schoolchildren could not now pronounce, and he had understood before he was nine years old the thing the entire libertarian internet had never once grasped.

A measure without a keeper is not free. It is unguarded.

"They'll find out," the voice said. "There's a migration proposal. Two of them. There are people writing papers."

"There are always people writing papers."

"Teague was going to publish."

"Teague," said Aldrich Vane, "is a solved problem."

He said it without pleasure, aware that he had crossed a line he could not uncross, and that he would cross it again tomorrow, and that this was the difference between a man who wanted things and a man who was responsible for them.

"Sir — a complication. He had a family member listed."

Vane's eyes did not leave the brass. "As what?"

"Executor. And there's a dependency in the paperwork. He left instructions that require the man physically present."

"Name."

"Canna. Zeno Canna. Metrologist at NIST Boulder."

And for the first time in eleven years, Aldrich Vane put his hand flat on the museum glass, a thing he had sworn never to do, because the oil of a fingertip changes what it touches.

"A what?" he said.

3

Denver International · 8:20 a.m. Mountain

He packed in nine minutes because he had packed this bag in his head a hundred times without admitting it.

Two shirts. The Mitutoyo digital calipers, which were a toy and which he carried anyway, the way a retired priest carries a rosary. A steel rule. And, at the bottom, wrapped in a sock, a brass object the size of a cigarette lighter that his stepfather had given him for his eleventh birthday: a Gunter's chain link, one hundredth of a surveyor's chain, 7.92 inches, the unit that had measured out most of the property lines in the United States.

Everything you will ever own, Teague had said, somebody walked off with one of these.

At the gate Zeno did the thing he always did in airports and never told anyone about. He picked a stranger and estimated their height, then waited for them to stand near a door frame and checked himself against the frame.

Six one. He was two centimeters over.

He ran it again on a woman by the window. Five four. He was two and a half centimeters over.

Both errors in the same direction.

Zeno Canna sat very still in seat 14C with a paper cup going cold in his hand and understood that he was not calibrated this morning, and told himself it was grief, which is what people say when they are frightened of a number.

4

Suffolk County Police Headquarters · Yaphank, New York · 3:10 p.m. Eastern

Detective Mira Novak had eleven years and a hundred and four homicides and had never once had a family member ask her about distance.

"You want to explain to me," she said, "why you're not asking who shot him."

"Because you don't know," Zeno said, "and you won't for a while, and I can't help with that."

She looked at him. Late forties, maybe fifty. Dark, contained, badly rested, and holding himself like a man who was measuring the room and did not know he was doing it. He had glanced at the ceiling tiles twice since sitting down and she had a strong suspicion he now knew how tall the room was.

"He was shot near the shaft," she said. "He crawled about seventy, eighty feet toward the road. That's typical. People go toward the road."

"He didn't crawl."

"There's a drag pattern in the—"

"There's a walking pattern," Zeno said, "with a drag at the very end of it. Detective, I'd like to be wrong. I'd like it very much. Take me out there and let me be wrong."

Novak was quiet a moment.

"You know what's strange?" she said. "I've got a man shot twice at four in the morning at a decommissioned Tesla site, and the only thing missing from his car is a coat."

Zeno's head came up.

"A coat."

"Empty hanger in the back seat, dry cleaning tag from Tuesday. No coat. In April." She tilted her head. "Why does that make your face do that?"

"Because he never carried a briefcase," Zeno said. "He carried things in his coat."

5

Wardenclyffe · 3:52 p.m.

They had put up sixty feet of tape and parked the vans on the grass, and there were nine people on the site who all believed they were looking at a crime scene.

Zeno Canna stood at the edge of the octagon and saw a sentence.

"He fell there." Novak pointed. "Coroner puts it around four fifteen."

Zeno crouched without touching anything. He didn't need to touch anything.

"Look at the impressions. Left deeper than the right by roughly the same margin the whole way — that's a man holding a wound on his left side and shifting his weight off it. Now look at the spacing." He glanced up at her. "Look how even it is. A dying man's stride collapses, Detective. It shortens every step. His didn't. He was holding the length on purpose."

Novak looked at the grass and saw grass.

"Okay," she said slowly. "So what."

"So he wasn't running." Zeno stood. "He was writing."

He stepped to the lip of the shaft where the blood pool started, set his heel against the concrete, and began to walk the line his stepfather had walked eleven hours earlier.

He knew the stride. He had learned to walk from this man. He had learned to walk this way from this man, at seven years old, in a driveway in Rocky Point, counting out loud while Ambrose Teague pretended to be impressed and then, in the second week, stopped pretending.

One. Two. Three.

The site went quiet behind him. Even the ones who thought he was insane went quiet, because there is something about a man walking a straight line with his eyes closed that stops conversation.

Forty. Forty-one.

He felt the ground change where the old rail spur had been. He felt the slope Tesla graded a hundred and twenty-five years ago to move copper he never got to use.

Sixty-six. Sixty-seven.

His foot came down on the sixty-eighth pace, his heel found the exact edge of the stain, and Zeno Canna opened his eyes and did not breathe for a while.

"Sixty-eight," Novak said. "Is that supposed to mean something?"

"Not by itself." His voice came out wrong. He cleared it. "A pace isn't a number. A pace is a number times a body. And he knew mine."

He knelt at the stain. Half-filled with eleven hours of dew, pressed into the sand by two fingers, was the character.

Novak leaned in. "That Arabic?"

"Aramaic."

"Okay. What's it say?"

Zeno stared at the little hooked shape in the dirt — the shape of a word that in the oldest surviving version of the most-spoken prayer on earth does the work of two English words at once — and felt the floor of his life tilt about four degrees.

"It's the one they mistranslate," he said.

"As what?"

He looked up at her.

"Trespasses." He wiped his mouth. "It actually means debts."

6

Wardenclyffe · 4:40 p.m.

"Debts," Novak repeated. "He's bleeding out and he writes debts."

"He writes it in a language nobody's spoken conversationally in twelve hundred years, in a script four people in this county can read, with two fingers, in the dark." Zeno was pacing now — not measuring, just moving. "That's not a dying thought. That's a courier."

"To you."

"To whoever could walk sixty-eight paces and land on the right spot." He stopped. "Which is me."

Novak wrote nothing down, which he noticed, and which he decided he approved of.

"So what's the debt?" she said.

"That's the wrong question."

"Enlighten me."

"He didn't write a debt. He wrote the word." Zeno turned toward the shaft, toward the flat gray octagon and the weeds. "Ambrose spent forty years on standards. Units. Definitions. He didn't care about things, he cared about the words for things and what happens when they slip. If he used a word, he meant the word."

Novak folded her arms. "You're telling me your stepfather's last act on this earth was a vocabulary lesson."

"I'm telling you," Zeno said, "that he thought he had time for exactly one word, and out of every word in every language he knew, he picked the one that means two things at once."

Behind them a tech called out that they were ready to move the body.

Zeno said, "Wait — sixty-eight paces from the shaft. What's under my feet?"

The tech looked down. "Grass?"

Zeno knelt and pulled the weeds apart with both hands, and there in the dirt, green-black with a century of oxide, was a brass survey disk stamped L.I.R.R. SPUR · 1903 · BM 4.

A benchmark.

"He didn't fall here," Zeno said quietly. "He arrived here."

7

Zurich · 11:20 p.m. Central European Time

Dr. Sibilla Roux had sent Ambrose Teague nine emails in six weeks and received two replies, both of them one line long, both of them from a man who had never in his life written a one-line reply.

The first said: Not by mail.

The second said: If I stop, look at the artifacts.

She had read the second one perhaps sixty times, and had concluded, each time, that it was either a typo or the beginning of something that would end her career.

At 11:20 she was still at the department, running a lattice reduction against a parameter set she was no longer supposed to have, when the alert came up on her second screen — a name search she had left running for six weeks and forgotten about.

Suffolk County, NY. Homicide. Victim identified as Ambrose Teague, 71.

Sibilla Roux sat back slowly.

Then she killed the job, wiped the scratch disk, took the stairs instead of the elevator, and was out of the building in under four minutes, which she would think about later, because it meant that some part of her had been ready for this for weeks.

Yaphank · 6:15 p.m.

They brought Zeno the personal effects in a plastic tray, and the third item was a folded sheet of paper in an evidence sleeve, soft as cloth with age.

"Found in his sock," Novak said. "Which is a thing I've seen twice in eleven years, both times drug money."

Zeno turned on the desk lamp.

It was a page from a contract. Heavy rag stock, the kind that outlives everyone who touches it. Letterhead: WESTINGHOUSE ELECTRIC & MANUFACTURING COMPANY, PITTSBURGH, PA. Dated 1897.

Royalty schedule. Two dollars and fifty cents per horsepower of alternating current sold.

The signature block at the bottom had been torn away. Not cut. Torn, along a line that ran diagonally through the last paragraph, taking half of a printed clause with it.

Zeno felt something move in his chest that had nothing to do with grief.

"Do you know what this is?" he said.

"Old paperwork."

"This is the most expensive piece of paper in American history." He didn't look up. "In 1897, George Westinghouse was being squeezed by his own investors. They told him the company would survive if he could get out from under the royalty he owed on the polyphase patents. Which he couldn't. Because the man who held them had a contract, and the contract was worth — in modern money — somewhere between ten and thirty billion dollars."

Novak's eyebrows went up half a centimeter.

"So Tesla went to Pittsburgh," Zeno said, "and Westinghouse told him the truth, and Nikola Tesla took the contract off the table and tore it up in front of him. Because the alternative was that alternating current died in committee." He finally looked at Novak. "He gave away the largest fortune of the twentieth century to keep the technology alive, and he died broke in a hotel room on Thirty-Fourth Street forty-six years later feeding pigeons."

Novak was silent for a second.

"And your stepfather kept a page of it in his sock."

"No," Zeno said. "He kept half of it in his sock."

JFK, Terminal 4 · 9:02 a.m. the following morning

She was waiting by the wrong carousel, which is how Zeno noticed her, because everyone else in the hall was facing the belts and she was facing the doors.

"Dr. Canna."

"Do I know you?"

"No." She put out her hand. "Sibilla Roux. ETH Zurich. Your father emailed me eleven times in the last two years and I never met him and I got on a plane six hours after I found out he was dead, so I'm going to skip the part where we establish that this is strange."

"Stepfather."

"He signed his emails your friend Ambrose," she said. "I assumed a lot of things."

Zeno looked at her. Forty, maybe. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with the flight.

"What did he write to you about?"

"Lattices."

"I don't know what that means."

"Yes you do," Sibilla said. "You just don't know that you do. It means the math that's going to replace all the math, in about six years, because the current math is going to stop working." She glanced around the hall once. "Dr. Canna, is there somewhere we could not be standing in an arrivals terminal."

"Why?"

"Because I flew here to tell you that your stepfather thought someone was going to kill him," she said, "and I've spent the last six hours trying to work out how to say that gently, and I can't."

10

Diner, Route 25A, Rocky Point · 10:40 a.m.

She talked for forty minutes and Zeno understood maybe half.

"Every secure thing you have ever touched," she said, "your bank, your passport, your phone, the update that installed itself last night — it's all standing on one bet. That certain problems are hard. Factoring. Discrete logs on elliptic curves. Hard enough that all the computers on earth couldn't get there before the sun burned out."

"And they're not hard."

"They're not hard for a quantum machine." She turned her coffee cup a quarter turn. "Shor's algorithm, 1994. Peter Shor wrote down a method that solves both of those problems in polynomial time on hardware nobody could build. For thirty years it was a curiosity. Now it's a schedule."

"How long?"

"Ask nine people, get nine answers. But the ones who actually build the machines have stopped saying decades."

Zeno turned the salt shaker around. "So the world changes the locks."

"The world changes the locks. That's exactly right, and it's why I know your father was killed." She leaned in. "Dr. Canna, somebody has to decide what the new locks are. There's a process. It's public, it's slow, it's run out of a standards institute, and it is the single most consequential committee that has ever existed, and almost nobody outside my field could name it."

Zeno set the salt shaker down.

"NIST," he said.

"NIST."

"I work at NIST."

"I know," said Sibilla Roux. "So did he. That's not the coincidence. That's the reason."

11

The same diner · 11:05 a.m.

"Explain it to me," Zeno said, "like I'm a man who measures things."

"Fine." She pushed her plate aside. "You keep the meter."

"Roughly."

"Not roughly, exactly — you keep the meter. And because you keep it, every bridge in the world is built to your number. You don't own the bridges. You don't build them. But if the number is wrong by a hair, every bridge is wrong by a hair, and nobody finds out until something falls." Sibilla spread her hands. "Now imagine the meter is a signature scheme, and the bridges are every bank, army, hospital, and currency on the planet, and the number is being chosen next year."
